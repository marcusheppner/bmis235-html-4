Monologues & streams of consciousness

How these differ from the scenes above: the scenes are what happens. These are the passages where the narration sinks into one mind (or, once, where the narrator speaks out directly). The quoted line is there to help you find each passage in your copy. Monologues are listed in the order they appear, and each one links to the scene it belongs to.

M1
Clarissa · Opening, Westminster · see scene 01
What a lark! What a plunge!

The June morning in 1923 pulls her back to Bourton at eighteen. It's the first example of how the book moves between present and past in a single breath.

Breakdown →
M2
Clarissa · Walking to Bond Street · see scene 01
She sliced like a knife through everything; at the same time was outside, looking on.

Clarissa describes her own split self: totally involved in life and totally apart from it. This idea comes back in every theme about isolation.

Breakdown →
M3
Clarissa · Bond Street · see scene 01
...this being Mrs. Dalloway; not even Clarissa any more; this being Mrs. Richard Dalloway.

She feels invisible, defined entirely by marriage and class. This is a key passage for identity and for what conformity costs her.

Breakdown →
M4
Clarissa · Hatchards' shop window · see scene 01
Fear no more the heat o' the sun / Nor the furious winter's rages.

The Shakespeare line first appears here as she thinks about dying and somehow surviving in other people and places. Septimus will think the same words later.

Breakdown →
M5
Clarissa · The attic room · see scene 04
...the most exquisite moment of her whole life passing a stone urn with flowers in it.

Her memory of Sally's kiss, set beside her "narrow" bed and her coldness toward Richard. This is the central passage for desire and repression.

Breakdown →
M6
Peter · Asleep in Regent's Park · see scene 06
The solitary traveller

Peter's half-dream of a traveler meeting a vast, comforting female figure. It's dreamlike and symbolic, and it's one of the hardest passages to read on a first pass.

Breakdown →
M7
Peter · Memory of Bourton · see scene 06
The death of her soul.

Peter remembers judging Clarissa's prudishness at Bourton. It shows how he sees her, and how harshly he judges her.

Breakdown →
M8
Septimus · Regent's Park · see scene 07
Men must not cut down trees. There is a God.

His "messages" to the world, the sparrows singing in Greek, and Evans behind the railings. It's madness and prophecy at once, and it's essential for the disability lens.

Breakdown →
M9
Septimus · Memory of the war and after · see scene 08
Human nature, in short, was on him.

He comes to see all of humanity, and the doctors in particular, as brutal. Shakespeare, he decides, "loathed humanity." His trauma becomes a judgment of the whole world.

Breakdown →
M10
Rezia · By the fountain in Regent's Park · see scene 07
I am alone; I am alone!

The caregiver's own inner life: homesickness for Milan, exhaustion, love. It's easy to skip, and it's important for disability readings.

Breakdown →
M11
The narrator · After Bradshaw's appointment · see scene 09
Proportion, divine proportion, Sir William's goddess...

The narrator steps out of any one character's head to attack Bradshaw's "Proportion" and "Conversion" directly. It's the novel's most openly angry passage about medical power.

Breakdown →
M12
Miss Kilman · Army & Navy Stores · see scene 12
The flesh...

Her hunger, envy, religious fervor, and humiliation as Elizabeth slips away. It's a study of a body and a mind pushed to the margins.

Breakdown →
M13
Clarissa · After Miss Kilman leaves · see scene 12
...the privacy of the soul.

Clarissa watches the old lady in the house opposite climbing her stairs and decides that love and religion both destroy the privacy of the soul. Her deepest belief, stated plainly.

Breakdown →
M14
Elizabeth · On the omnibus up the Strand · see scene 13
She would become a doctor, a farmer, possibly go into Parliament...

A young woman imagining a career her mother never had. It's a short, hopeful counterpoint to the rest of the book.

Breakdown →
M15
Septimus · At the window · see scene 15
He did not want to die. Life was good. The sun hot.

His last thoughts before Holmes reaches the door. It's crucial for arguing whether his death is despair, defiance, or something else.

Breakdown →
M16
Clarissa · The little room at the party · see scene 18
Death was defiance. Death was an attempt to communicate...

Clarissa reflects on a stranger's death, then feels "very like him" and goes back to her guests. This is where the novel's two halves meet.

Breakdown →
M17
Peter · The final lines · see scene 19
What is this terror? what is this ecstasy?

The book ends inside Peter's mind as Clarissa enters: "For there she was." Ask what it means to end on her presence rather than her thoughts.
